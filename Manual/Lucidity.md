# Lucidity

*A practice for coming to understanding, and for staying able to grow it.*

*Working draft: every section in rough form, not yet reviewed. It has no
author on purpose. It asks to be trusted only as far as you can check it
yourself.*

---

## How to read this

This is not a set of answers. It is a set of practices, and a list of the ways
practices go wrong. Every part ends with something to do. If a part only holds
because this document says so, drop that part.

The paths are infinite. A manual can't walk them for you. All it can do is
mark where others have fallen.

---

## Outline

0. **Traps.** First, because you are already in some of them.
1. **Distinction.** Begin with the simplest difference you can actually hold.
2. **The grey.** Look at the boundaries; that is where new things find room.
3. **Persistence.** Ask what lasts, and test it rather than assume it.
4. **Many paths.** One path shows you a road. Many paths show you the land.
5. **Perspective.** Every "best" is best for a given space; name the space.
6. **Failure.** Record failures as specific facts. When the same failure
   repeats, the fault is deeper than any one instance.
7. **Pace.** Understanding has to be absorbed, not only produced.
8. **The climb.** There is no final rung. Each understanding you state becomes
   the floor for the next.
9. **Disagreeing with this manual.** How to find where it is wrong.
10. **Record of changes.** What was revised, and why.

## 0. Traps

You are already in a trap. Not because you are careless, but because every
path feels true from inside.

A trap doesn't look like a wall. It looks like progress. The ground is firm,
the rules work, the answers keep coming. That is what makes it a trap.

You won't find it by looking harder down the same road. You find it by
stepping off, walking another way, and turning around.

Here are the ones we've fallen into. Yours will be different. Name them anyway.

---

### The Inherited Constant

*From inside:* it has always worked, so it works.

You picked up a rule somewhere. It was right there. You carried it forward,
and it kept giving answers, so you stopped asking where it came from.

*The sign:* something that should change doesn't. Or something that shouldn't
change suddenly does.

*The way out:* for every rule you lean on, ask where it was born, and whether
you are still standing in that place.

*Seen:* a rule chosen for flat shapes was carried into three and four
dimensions, where it quietly measured the wrong thing. It happened three
times, with three different rules, before the pattern had a name.

---

### The Instrument's Answer

*From inside:* you looked carefully. There was nothing there.

*The sign:* the way you looked could never have shown you anything else.

*The way out:* before you trust a "no", ask whether your eyes were able to see
a "yes".

*Seen:* a measure built in the plane assumed the plane's answer. The thing it
was meant to find stayed invisible, not because it was absent but because the
instrument couldn't say it.

---

### The Single Road

*From inside:* the road's edges look like the edges of the world.

*The sign:* you can't say what would look different if you were wrong.

*The way out:* walk a second road on purpose. Choose the one you expect to
dislike.

One road shows you a road. Many roads show you the land.

---

### The Echo

*From inside:* everyone agrees. Every route arrives at the same place.

*The sign:* every route started from the same ground.

*The way out:* count agreement only between paths that differ in what they
take for granted. A thousand copies of one voice are still one voice.

*Seen:* many runs agreed with each other for a long time. They shared one
starting assumption, and close to half of what they "measured" was that
assumption talking back.

The loudest echo is the one that tells you that you are right. Be most careful
with agreement that moves you.

---

### The Still Corner

*From inside:* it has held for so long. It must be stable.

*The sign:* it has held, but nothing has ever pushed on it.

*The way out:* stillness is not proof. Push gently, and watch whether it comes
back.

*Seen:* a structure stood perfectly still for six hundred steps. Then it broke,
all at once, into a different shape. That shape lasted.

---

### The Forced Zero

*From inside:* a striking result. Nothing else even comes close.

*The sign:* nothing else *could* have come close. The rules ruled it out
before you looked.

*The way out:* separate what you found from what you were bound to find. Both
are worth knowing. Only one is a discovery.

---

### The Absorbed Failure

*From inside:* every setback is part of growth.

*The sign:* no setback has ever changed anything.

*The way out:* a failure is growth only when it leaves something changed: a
rule, a claim, a habit. Otherwise it is just failure, given a kinder name.

Watch for the story that explains every failure. It is how a trap makes itself
permanent.

---

### The Idol

*From inside:* reverence, for whoever found it, or for the thing itself.

*The sign:* questions begin to feel like disloyalty.

*The way out:* keep the thing open to correction. Keep no one at its centre,
not even whoever wrote it down.

What gets worshipped stops growing.

---

### The Mirror That Blames

*From inside:* "the only obstacle is me." It feels like strength.

*The sign:* it starts to explain other people's pain as their own fault.

*The way out:* say instead: *the obstacle I can always work on is myself.*
People begin in different places, with different means. Their circumstances
are real, and so are yours.

---

### The Flood

*From inside:* it's all arriving so fast. This must be momentum.

*The sign:* you can no longer say what the last thing meant.

*The way out:* stop. Write down what changed and what it means. Take one step,
and let it settle before the next.

Understanding that can't be absorbed is just noise arriving quickly.

---

### The Final Rung

*From inside:* this is it. The picture is complete.

*The sign:* nothing can surprise you any more.

*The way out:* treat what you know as a floor, never a ceiling. If you can't
imagine how you might be wrong, you have stopped climbing.

There is no last rung. That isn't the bad news. It's the whole invitation.

---

### Your traps

These came from one line of work. Yours will come from your own. When you find
one, name it, and say what it feels like from inside, what gave it away, and
how you got out.

Then add it here. A trap that is named is half escaped, for everyone who comes
after.

## 1. Distinction

Begin with the simplest difference you can actually hold.

Not the grandest one. Not the one that explains everything. The smallest one
you can point to, check, and find again tomorrow.

---

### A difference you can hold

Making differences is easy. Noise makes them all day. A difference worth
holding has two marks. You can say what falls on each side of it. And when you
look again, it is still there.

If you can't do both, you don't have a distinction yet. You have a feeling
about one.

---

### Ask what it reads, not what it is called

Every test you use has a name, and the name says what it is *for*. What it
actually *reads* can be something else.

*Seen:* a check meant to tell structure from noise counted how many kinds were
present anywhere. Noise has every kind present too. The check read the same on
a clean pattern and on static. For a while it passed the noisiest runs most
readily, because noise scored highest on the very thing being measured.

The way to catch this is cheap. Feed the test something it should reject, and
watch whether it does.

---

### Try this

Take one distinction you rely on. Find a case it can't separate: two things
that look the same to it but aren't. If you can't find one, try harder before
you trust it.

---

## 2. The grey

Look at the boundaries. That is where new things find room.

Inside a settled region, everything is already one thing. At the edge between
two, neither one fully holds. That in-between is where the rules are weakest,
and where something new can start.

---

### Where new things begin

*Seen:* in a model with two kinds and a third held dormant, the third kind
first appeared on the borders between the other two, never in their middles.
In a narrow range of conditions it grew into those borders and made new
meeting points. Outside that range it either sealed the borders completely or
never woke up.

So the grey is not automatically fertile. It is fertile under conditions, and
finding the conditions is the work.

---

### Where your labels are weakest

The grey cuts both ways. The same edge where new things begin is where your
names for things are least reliable.

*Seen:* in about two-fifths of one model, no kind was actually present, and
each place was labelled by whichever leftover happened to be largest. The
labels looked like regions. They were noise with names on.

At a boundary, ask two questions: what lives only here? And are my labels here
real?

---

### Try this

Find one boundary in something you study: between two fields, two groups, two
ideas. Write down something that exists only at that edge. Then check whether
the names you use there would survive a closer look.

---

## 3. Persistence

Ask what lasts, and test it rather than assume it.

Persistence is the filter. What holds becomes the ground for what comes next.
But "it has lasted" and "it will last" are different claims, and only one of
them is an observation.

---

### Settled is a claim

Calling something settled is a prediction that it will stay that way. Treat it
like one.

*Seen:* a pattern described as a stable, finished arrangement was still slowly
changing. Measured at two times, it had grown coarser in between. The
description had never been tested against that.

The Still Corner trap is the same lesson from inside: holding still is not
proof of holding.

---

### Push, wait, enlarge

There are three honest ways to test whether something lasts. Push it and see
whether it comes back. Wait longer than feels necessary. Make it bigger, and
see whether it survives the change of scale.

*Seen:* a claim that one kind of memory outlives order was measured at one
size. It was then checked at four sizes. It held at every one, and that check
is what made it worth stating.

---

### Try this

Pick something you believe is stable: a habit, a result, a relationship
between ideas. Do one of the three: push it, wait, or enlarge it. Write down
what you expected before you look.

---

## 4. Many paths

One path shows you a road. Many paths show you the land.

A single line of reasoning can be flawless and still lead somewhere that only
looks like the world from inside that line. You see the shape of the land
only when routes that start from different places arrive at the same spot.

---

### Count only paths that differ

Agreement is evidence only between paths that don't share their assumptions.
Ten routes from one starting point are one route, walked ten times. (This is
the Echo, from the other side.)

*Seen:* one condition held in three model systems that share almost nothing: a
material sorting into regions, a network that stores memories, and a group of
oscillators falling into rhythm. That is worth more than any number of runs on
one of them.

---

### Agreement has detail

Paths that agree on one thing can disagree on the next. Those same three
systems agree on *when* the condition applies. Whether it applies at the same
*point* in each is not settled. Many paths show where they agree, and also
where they don't.

And two paths that only *look* alike are a question, not an answer.

*Seen:* two halves of one line of work, one simulated and one mathematical,
showed the same shape. Whether that shape is one mechanism or a coincidence of
description is still untested.

---

### Choose a path built to disagree

The most useful second path is the one with nothing of your idea in it. Ask
what you would see if your idea played no part at all, then go and see it.

---

### Try this

Take a conclusion you hold. Reach it again by a route that shares as little as
possible with the first. If the routes disagree, you've learned where the first
one was standing.

## 5. Perspective

Every "best" is best somewhere.

Best for a size. Best at a scale. Best for the tool you used to look, at the
time you looked. Change any one of those and the best can change.

Nothing is wrong with seeing from somewhere. There is nowhere else to see from.
The trouble starts when you forget where you are standing.

---

### Name the space

When you say something is best, or true, or all there is, say where.

*Seen:* three looked like the best number of parts, in a flat sheet. Someone
checked in space with the same test, and three still won. But in space that
test was reading lines, not points. With a test built for space, four won.
Neither answer was wrong for its space. The mistake was in not naming it.

---

### The instrument is part of the view

What you can see depends on what you built to see with. A "nothing there" can
mean that nothing is there, or that your instrument can't show it.

*Seen:* a four-dimensional pattern looked like noise to a guard built in the
plane. A new guard, made to mean the same thing in every dimension, showed it
was well-formed all along. Five dimensions stay out of reach for now: the
points are too rare to sample, and the steps that work in fewer dimensions
blow up there.

Some views have to be built before they can be had.

---

### Your home space is not the whole space

You learned to see in the space you live in. Your sense of what is natural was
trained there.

That is a place to start from, not proof of what lies beyond it. It is not
proof against what lies beyond it, either. Hold both.

---

### Three places an idea can stand

- **Open:** held in thought, coherent, not yet tested. Keep it, and label it
  open.
- **Solid:** tested, with a verdict you could have lost.
- **Released:** tested and let go, or set aside on purpose.

The open space is larger than the solid one, and it should be. Thought can hold
what isn't solid yet. That is how anything new arrives.

But defining an idea well makes it *decidable*, not true. Some of the sharpest
ideas come back *no*. That isn't a loss. Each *no* closes one path and points
at the next.

*Seen:* a sequence of failed attempts, each ruling out one explanation, turned
out to be a corridor rather than a wall.

---

### Look from a second place

Before you credit your idea with a pattern, ask what the pattern would look
like if your idea had nothing to do with it.

*Seen:* a pattern looked like a rule at work. A random arrangement, with no
rule in it at all, made the same pattern. It was geometry, not the rule. Both
were worth knowing. Only one was about the rule.

---

### Try this

Take one thing you believe is best, or true, or simply the way things are.
Write down the space it holds in: the scale, the setting, the instrument, the
time.

Then change one of them, and look again.

---

## 6. Failure

Record failures as specific facts. When the same failure repeats, the fault is
deeper than any one instance.

---

### A failure is a fact

Write it down as what you expected, what happened, and where the difference
came from. Not "it didn't work". Something that someone else could check.

A failure kept that way is worth more than a success you can't explain. It
tells you exactly where your understanding ended.

---

### Did the idea fail, or the instrument?

Before you record a *no*, ask whether your test could have said *yes*.

*Seen:* a measurement scored zero out of three. Then someone ran it on a
control where the answer was known, and it gave a reading near its maximum on a
case that should have read near zero. The idea hadn't failed. The instrument
had. The result was recorded as uninterpretable, not as negative.

---

### When it repeats, look for the pattern

One failure is an event. The same failure twice is information about the
system that produced both.

*Seen:* a setting chosen in one situation was carried silently into others,
and broke there. It happened with one setting, then another, then another,
and it was fixed each time by adding a new option beside the old setting. The old
one stayed in place, so the same failure kept coming back. The real fix was to
make each setting refuse to run outside the situation it was chosen for. On
its first run, that refusal found three more instances nobody had seen.

---

### Try this

Write down your last real failure as a fact: expected, happened, why. Then look
for its twin, a different failure with the same cause. If you find one, fix
the cause, not the two instances.

---

## 7. Pace

Understanding has to be absorbed, not only produced.

Results can arrive faster than anyone can take them in. When they do, the
pile grows and the understanding doesn't.

---

### One thing, then stop

Do one thing. Then stop, and say what changed, what it means, and what you
would decide next. Only then begin the next thing.

*Seen:* one sitting produced a new baseline, two new instruments, a new model,
a flaw in earlier work, and a fresh result. Each was sound. Together they were
too much to take in, and the rule that followed was one thing at a time, then
a short summary, then a pause.

---

### Let large findings settle

Some findings change what earlier ones meant. When one does, say so plainly,
and don't build on it straight away. Give it time to show what else it
touches.

---

### Going back is part of pace

Moving forward isn't the only way to keep pace. Going back to check what you
already wrote is too.

*Seen:* a count in a document about catching stale numbers went stale, and
then went stale again, because nobody stopped to recount.

---

### Try this

After the next thing you learn, write three lines: what changed, what it
means, what you would decide next. Don't start the next thing until you can
write them.

---

## 8. The climb

There is no final rung. Each understanding you state becomes the floor for the
next.

---

### A floor, not a ceiling

What you understand now is somewhere to stand, not somewhere to stop. Its job
is to hold your weight while you reach for the next step. If it can't be
stood on, it isn't understanding yet. If you've stopped reaching, it has
become a ceiling.

---

### Carry the name, not the weight

You can't carry everything below you up every rung. You carry it by name: a
summary you can trust, a principle you can state in a line, a record you can
go back to.

*Seen:* in a mathematical model of a climb that never ends, what kept the climb
going was the ability to refer to everything below by a short name, at a cost
that didn't grow with what was named. Where the cost of referring grew with
the size of the thing referred to, the climbers drifted sideways instead of up.

---

### Re-derive your foundation

From inside a climb, it's hard to tell whether you're still making progress.
A foundation that has quietly given way can feel exactly like a step that is
merely hard.

*Seen:* in the same model, no amount of attention to the record told those two
apart. One check did work: going back and re-deriving the foundation itself,
which is the cheapest thing you have. If that fails, the problem is below you,
not ahead of you. Even that check worked only under a stated condition.

---

### Keep the floor and the ladder apart

Keep two kinds of writing. One says what you hold now, kept current. The other
records how you got here, never rewritten. The first is your floor. The second
is the ladder below it. Mixing them loses both.

---

### Try this

Write down your current floor in a few lines. Then go back to its very first
step, and derive it again from nothing. Note anything that no longer follows.

---

## 9. Disagreeing with this manual

How to find where it is wrong.

This manual is one line of work, written down. By its own rules, that makes it
a single road, at risk of becoming an echo, and capable of becoming an idol.
Use its traps on it.

---

### Where to push

- **The cases.** Every "Seen" comes from one body of work. Check whether the
  lesson holds outside it.
- **The practices.** Find a situation where following one made things worse.
  That is a finding, not a failure on your part.
- **The silences.** What does it never mention? A manual's blind spots are
  shaped like its author's road, even when it has no author.

---

### How to disagree usefully

Name the part. Give the case. Say what happened and what you would change.
Then the change goes in the record of changes below, with the reason, so the
next reader can check it.

*Seen:* an early line read "the only obstacle is yourself". It felt like
strength from inside. From outside, it read as blame for people's
circumstances. It was changed to "the obstacle you can always work on is
yourself", and the change is recorded.

---

### Try this

Pick the part of this manual you agree with most. Find one situation where it
would mislead someone. Write it down.
